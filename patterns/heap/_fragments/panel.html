1. Always know who is next, without sorting everyone

Many problems keep asking the same question: "which item is the best one right now?" Items arrive and leave as you go.

Sorting answers it once, but costs a full sort, and new items break the order. A heap answers it again and again, cheaply, as items come and go.

Analogy: a hospital triage desk. Patients do not queue in arrival order. The nurse only needs to know who is the most urgent right now. A new patient is slotted in by urgency; when a doctor is free, the most urgent goes in. Nobody ranks the whole waiting room from first to last.

Try it: the 3 largest of 10 numbers, two ways

The list is [4, 9, 2, 7, 1, 8, 3, 6, 5, 0]. Press Step or Play on each player and watch the comparison counter.

Pattern: keep a heap of only 3. Its top is the weakest of the best 3 so far. Each new number is compared with the top: bigger ones replace it, smaller ones are skipped in one comparison.

Slow: sort everything. A merge sort puts all 10 in order, then takes the last 3. Each frame is one comparison; the blue cells are the part being merged.

Both counts come from running the two methods on this list, in the page and in measure.py (13 and 25). On 10 numbers the gap is small; section 7 races them on up to 100,000.

Rule to rememberWhen you only ever need the best one (or the best k), do not sort. Keep a heap: peek at the top in O(1), add or remove in O(log n).

3. Mental map: from a blank page to working code

Read this top to bottom when a problem lands in front of you. It is the "how do I start" checklist.

  1. Signal"k largest / smallest / closest / most frequent", "k-th largest", a stream of numbers arriving one by one, "repeatedly take the best and put something back", or several sorted lists to combine.
  2. Ask"Do I need the whole order, or only the best item, again and again, while the set keeps changing?" If only the best, a heap. Then: "smallest or largest on top?" and "what is the priority of each item?"
  3. PickChoose the variant by its clue in yellow.
    • "k largest", "k closest"Top-k: a heap of size k whose top is the weakest of the best. For k largest use a min-heap. Kth Largest Element, K Closest Points.
    • k sorted lists or streamsK-way merge: the heap holds one front item per list as (value, list id, index). Merge k Sorted Lists.
    • "median" of a streamTwo heaps: a max-heap for the lower half and a min-heap for the upper half, kept within one item in size. Find Median from Data Stream.
    • always do the best next, then it changesGreedy with a heap: pop the best, do it, push back what remains. Last Stone Weight, Task Scheduler (and later, Dijkstra's shortest path).
  4. InvariantEvery parent is at most its children, so h[0] is the minimum. For top-k: the heap holds the k best seen so far, and h[0] is the k-th best.
  5. StepsDecide the priority, and negate it if you need the largest first. Build the heap (heapify or pushes). Loop: peek or pop the top, act, push new items. For top-k, push and pop when the size goes over k, or heapreplace when a new item beats the top.
  6. CheckMin or max? Python only has a min-heap; negate numbers for a max-heap (Trap 1). For k largest the heap must be a min-heap (Trap 2).
  7. CheckTuples compare item by item. Can two priorities tie? Add a counter so Python never compares the payloads (Trap 3).
  8. CheckEdge inputs: k equal to n, k = 1, duplicates, negative numbers, an empty heap before a pop.
Rule to rememberName the priority, pick min or max, and ask whether you can keep only k items. The rest is heappush and heappop.

Template B: inside the heap (play with it)

The tree is stored level by level in a plain list. Node i has its parent at (i - 1) // 2 and children at 2i + 1 and 2i + 2. Push and pop each fix one path from top to bottom, so they cost at most one swap per level.

Template C: top-k with a heap of size k

CodeWhat it means
heap = []A min-heap that will hold the best k seen so far.
for x in nums:One pass over the data. It also works on a stream.
if len(heap) < k: heapq.heappush(heap, x)Fill up to k items.
elif x > heap[0]:Does x beat the weakest of the best? One comparison; most items fail it and are skipped.
heapq.heapreplace(heap, x)Throw out the weakest, let x in. O(log k).
return heapThe k largest, in heap order. heap[0] is the k-th largest.

For "k smallest" or "k closest", flip it: keep a max-heap of size k (store -distance) so the worst of the best is on top.

Template D: merge k sorted lists

CodeWhat it means
heap = [(lst[0], i, 0) for i, lst in enumerate(lists) if lst]The front item of every list, with which list and which position it came from.
heapq.heapify(heap)The smallest front item is now on top.
val, i, j = heapq.heappop(heap)Take the overall smallest.
if j + 1 < len(lists[i]): heappush(heap, (lists[i][j + 1], i, j + 1))Its list sends its next item. The heap never holds more than k items.
 
merge_k([[1, 4, 7], [2, 5, 8], [3, 6, 9]]) = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]  pops = 9  (heap never holds more than 3)

Template E: two heaps for a running median

Keep the smaller half in a max-heap low and the bigger half in a min-heap high. Every new number passes through low into high, so the halves stay in order; then the sizes are rebalanced. The middle is always on the tops.

medians of [5, 15, 1, 3, 8] = [5, 10.0, 5, 4.0, 5]
Rule to rememberA heap of size k for top-k. One item per list for k-way merge. Two heaps facing each other for a median. Pop the best, push back the rest for greedy scheduling.

5. Spot the pattern: does a heap help here?

Read each made-up task. Decide: heap or not, and which variant? Then reveal.

A. A food app has 50,000 restaurants with map positions. Show the 10 nearest to the user.

Yes: top-k. Signal: "the k nearest". Keep a max-heap of size 10 by distance (store -dist), so the farthest of the 10 is on top. O(n log 10) instead of sorting all 50,000.

B. Five servers each write a log file sorted by time. Produce one combined log, in time order.

Yes: k-way merge. Signal: several sorted lists into one. The heap holds the next line of each file: at most 5 items.

C. Temperatures arrive every minute. After each reading, report the middle value of all readings so far.

Yes: two heaps. Signal: a median over a stream. O(log n) per reading and O(1) to read the median.

D. Find the single tallest person in a list of heights.

No, not this pattern. You need the best item once, and the set never changes. One scan with max() is O(n) and needs no extra memory. A heap pays off only when you ask "who is best?" again and again as items come and go.

E. Print a list of exam scores from lowest to highest.

No, not this pattern. You want the whole order, all at once. Just call sorted(): O(n log n). Popping every item from a heap also sorts (heapsort), but it is no faster and more code.

F. A CPU has jobs of several types. Two jobs of the same type need a gap of n ticks. Finish them all as fast as possible, doing the most common type first.

Yes: greedy with a heap. Signal: "always do the best available next", and the best keeps changing. Pop the type with the most jobs left, run one, and park it until its cooldown ends. This is LeetCode 621, Task Scheduler.

Trigger signals"k largest / smallest / closest / frequent", "k-th", streams, merging sorted lists, a median that moves, and "repeatedly take the best". Not a heap: the best item once (scan), or the full order once (sort).

6. Four classic traps

Trap 1: expecting a max-heap

heapq only knows "smallest on top". Pop from it expecting the largest and you get the smallest.

heappop on [3, 1, 4] = 1   # wanted the max, 4
-heappop on negated  = 4

Fix: push -x and negate on the way out. For tuples, negate the priority only: (-count, word).

Trap 2: the wrong heap for top-k

For the k largest, the item to throw away is the smallest of your k. A max-heap throws away the biggest instead, and you keep the k smallest.

for x in nums:
    heapq.heappush(h, -x)        # wrong: a max-heap
    if len(h) > k: heapq.heappop(h)

k largest of [5, 1, 9, 3, 7], k = 2 with a max-heap: [1, 3]
k largest of [5, 1, 9, 3, 7], k = 2 with a min-heap: [7, 9]

Fix: k largest needs a min-heap; k smallest or closest needs a max-heap. The top is always the one you would throw out next.

Trap 3: ties that compare the payload

Tuples compare field by field. When two priorities tie, Python compares the next field. Dicts, ListNodes and other objects cannot be compared, so the push crashes.

heapq.heappush(h, (1, {"id": "a"}))
heapq.heappush(h, (1, {"id": "b"}))
push (1, dict) twice -> TypeError: '<' not supported between instances of 'dict' and 'dict'
with a counter: (1, 0, {'id': 'a'})

Fix: put a unique tie-breaker second: (prio, next(counter), item) with itertools.count(), or the list index as in Template D.

Trap 4: treating the heap as sorted

Only h[0] is promised. The rest of the list is in heap order, not sorted order.

heapify([5, 1, 4, 2, 3]) = [1, 2, 4, 5, 3]   h[:3] = [1, 2, 4]   true 3 smallest = [1, 2, 3]

Fix: to get items in order, pop them one by one, or use heapq.nsmallest(k, h).

Rule to rememberheapq is a min-heap; negate for max. The top is the item you throw out next. Break ties with a counter. Only h[0] is in order.

The heap's count is barely more than n: almost every number fails the one gate test and is skipped. The sort's count grows like n log n.

Step 1: why push and pop cost O(log n)

A complete tree doubles its width each level. With n items it has about log2 n levels below the root: for n = 1000, 9 levels (29 = 512 ≤ 1000 < 1024). Sift up climbs one path; sift down sinks one path. Each level costs at most one comparison going up, or two going down.

push: at most log2 n comparisons; pop: at most 2 x log2 n
Step 2: top-k costs O(n log k), not O(n log n)

The heap never holds more than k items, so each replace costs O(log k). Each item costs one gate comparison, plus O(log k) if it gets in. Total: O(n log k) time and O(k) memory. Sorting is O(n log n) time and O(n) memory. With k fixed and small, the heap is close to O(n).

The worst case for the heap is an increasing list: every item gets in. measure.py on [1..8] with k = 3 counts 17 heap comparisons against 12 for the sort. On tiny inputs the sort can win; the heap wins as n grows.

Step 3: the hidden cost, n pushes vs heapify

Building a heap by pushing n items one by one is O(n log n). heapify sifts down from the last parent to the root, and most nodes sit near the bottom with little room to sink, so it is O(n). Counted on a falling list (the worst case for pushes):

n =  1000: n pushes =   7987 comparisons   heapify =  1982 comparisons
n = 10000: n pushes = 113631 comparisons   heapify = 19982 comparisons

9. Your lesson's questions

Q1. Push 7, then 2, then 5 into an empty min-heap. What does the list look like?

[2, 7, 5]. 7 is the root. 2 goes to index 1, beats its parent 7, and swaps to the top. 5 goes to index 2; its parent is 2, so it stays. Not sorted, but a valid heap. measure.py prints the same. Try it in the playground.

Q2. A heap holds 1000 items. At most how many levels can a pushed item climb?

9. The tree has 9 levels below the root (29 = 512 ≤ 1000 < 210), so sift up does at most 9 swaps. That is the O(log n).

Q3. You want the 3 smallest numbers from a stream of a million. Which heap, of what size, and what is on top?

A max-heap of size 3 (push -x in Python). Its top is the largest of the 3 smallest so far: the one to throw out when a smaller number arrives. O(n log 3) time and O(3) memory.

Glossary: new words from this lesson

heap
A tree stored in a list where each parent beats its children, so the best item is at index 0.
priority queue
Add items in any order; always remove the best. Built with a heap.
min-heap / max-heap
Smallest on top / largest on top. Python's heapq is a min-heap.
root
The top of the heap, h[0].
complete binary tree
Filled level by level, left to right; so it fits in a list with no gaps.
parent / child index
Parent of i is (i - 1) // 2; children are 2i + 1 and 2i + 2.
sift up / sift down
Move an item up or down one path, swapping, until the heap order holds.
heapify
Turn a list into a heap in O(n).
top-k
Problems asking for the best k items; use a heap of size k.

Recap: the pattern card in one minute

Idea: a heap always hands you the smallest (or largest) item: peek in O(1), push and pop in O(log n). Use it when you need the best item again and again while the set changes.

Signals: k largest / smallest / closest / frequent, k-th, streams, merging sorted lists, a running median, "repeatedly take the best".

Templates: heappush, heappop, h[0], heapify. Top-k: a size-k heap whose top is the one to throw out. K-way merge: one item per list as (value, i, j). Median: two heaps.

Cost: top-k is O(n log k) time and O(k) memory; sorting is O(n log n). At n = 100,000 and k = 3: 100,025 comparisons against 1,536,355.

Traps: heapq is a min-heap (negate for max); the wrong heap direction for top-k; ties that compare payloads (add a counter); a heap is not sorted.

In the sandbox, type your own numbers and k, and watch the heap of size k decide each one. Try an increasing list to see the heap's worst case.
