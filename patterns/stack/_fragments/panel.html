Stack and Monotonic Stack

Goal: use a stack whenever the newest unfinished thing must be handled first. Then learn its sharper cousin, the monotonic stack, which answers "who is the next one taller than me?" for every item in a single pass.

1. The last thing in is the first thing out

A stack is a pile where you only touch the top. You add to the top and you take from the top.

That sounds limiting. It is exactly right whenever the newest unfinished thing must be finished first: a bracket that was opened last must be closed first, and the last action is the first one you undo.

Analogy: a pile of plates. Clean plates go on top. When you need one, you take the top one. You never pull a plate from the middle. The plate you put down last is the first one you use again.

Now a sharper use. People stand in a line, all facing right. For each person, who is the first taller person they can see? A short person standing right in front of a tall one is hidden by them. Once someone taller arrives, everyone shorter who was still waiting has their answer and can leave.

Analogy: a line of people waiting to see over the crowd. Keep a stack of the people still waiting. When a new person arrives, every waiting person shorter than them gets their answer and steps away. What stays on the stack always falls from tallest at the bottom to shortest on top. That shape is why it is called a monotonic stack.

Try it: the same line of people, two ways

Heights are [6, 2, 5, 1, 3, 4, 0, 8]. For each index, find the first taller height to its right, or -1. Press Step or Play on each player. Dimmed people already have their answer.

Pattern: monotonic stack. One walk left to right. Each new person pops every shorter person still waiting, then waits too.

Slow: walk right for each person. For every index, scan to the right one by one until someone taller shows up.

Look at the pushes and pops. However the line is shaped, every person is pushed once and popped at most once. That is why the stack never does more than about 2n comparisons. The nested scan has no such limit: on the falling line it does n x (n - 1) / 2.

Rule to rememberUse a stack when the most recent unfinished item is the one to deal with next. Use a monotonic stack when each item is waiting for "the next bigger" or "the next smaller" one.

3. Mental map: from a problem statement to a stack

Read this when a new problem feels stack-shaped but you are not sure how to start.

  1. SignalThings that open and must close in reverse order (brackets, tags, function calls). "Undo" or "go back". "For each item, the next bigger or smaller one", "how many days until warmer", "span", "histogram". Postfix expressions or a calculator.
  2. AskWhich earlier items are still unfinished, and is the newest of them the next one to settle? If yes, keep the unfinished items on a stack.
  3. Pick the variantEach variant has a tell-tale signal:
    • open and close in pairsMatching: push openers, a closer must match the top.
    • next greater, warmer, tallerMonotonic decreasing: pop while the top is smaller than the new item.
    • next smaller, widest rectangleMonotonic increasing: pop while the top is bigger. Often a 0 sentinel at the end.
    • tokens, operators, nesting like 3[ab]Expression: push numbers, an operator pops two and pushes the result.
    • stack plus a fast extra queryAugmented stack: store (value, min so far) pairs, or a second stack.
  4. InvariantEverything on the stack is still waiting for its answer, and (for monotonic stacks) the stack stays sorted from bottom to top.
  5. StepsWalk the input once. For each item: pop every top that this item settles, and record their answers. Then push the item (usually its index). After the loop, whatever is left never found an answer.
  6. CheckNever pop an empty stack. Decide what the leftovers mean (-1, 0, or "not valid"). Strict < or <= for equal values? Store indices when you need distances. In expressions, the first pop is the right operand.

4. Four templates

Template A: matching pairs

CodeWhat it means
def is_valid(s):Is every bracket closed by the right partner, in the right order?
pairs = {')': '(', ']': '[', '}': '{'}Each closer points to the opener it needs.

Template B: monotonic stack (next greater element)

CodeWhat it means
answer = [-1] * len(nums)Default for anyone who never finds a taller one.
stack = []Indices still waiting. Their heights fall from bottom to top.
for i, x in enumerate(nums):One pass. x is the new arrival.
while stack and nums[stack[-1]] < x:The top is shorter than x, so x is the first taller one it sees.
j = stack.pop()That index is settled and leaves for good.
answer[j] = xRecord its answer. For "how many days until warmer", write i - j instead.
stack.append(i)Now x waits for someone taller than itself.
return answerLeftovers keep -1.

Flip < to > and you get the next smaller element (an increasing stack). Walk from right to left, or read the new top after pushing, and you get the previous greater or smaller one.

From measure.py: ties give [5, 5, 5, -1] with 5 comparisons; falling gives all -1 with 0 pops; rising pops once per step.

Template C: evaluate an expression

CodeWhat it means
OPS = {'+': ..., '/': lambda a, b: int(a / b)}Each operator as a small function. int(a / b) cuts toward zero, which most problems ask for.
for t in tokens:Read tokens left to right.
if t in OPS:An operator needs the two newest numbers.
b = stack.pop() then a = stack.pop()The first pop is the right operand. Order matters for - and /.
stack.append(OPS[t](a, b))The result is a new number on the pile.
else: stack.append(int(t))A number waits on the stack.
return stack[0]One number is left: the answer.

The tokens 5 1 2 + 4 * + 3 - mean 5 + (1 + 2) x 4 - 3. measure.py prints 14.

Template D: a stack that also knows its minimum

CodeWhat it means
self.stack = []Store pairs: (value, smallest value at or below this one).
low = min(x, self.stack[-1][1]) if self.stack else xThe new minimum is x or the old minimum, whichever is smaller.
self.stack.append((x, low))Each plate carries a note: "the smallest plate from here down".
self.stack.pop()The pair below already knows its own minimum. Nothing to recompute.
return self.stack[-1][1]get_min is one look at the top: O(1).

Pattern words

5. Spot the pattern: stack or not?

Read each made-up task. Decide if a stack fits, and which variant. Then reveal.

A. A drawing app keeps every stroke. "Undo" removes the most recent stroke, again and again.

Yes, a plain stack. Signal: the newest action is undone first. Push each stroke, pop on undo.

B. You have a week of share prices. For each day, how many days until the price is higher than today?

Yes, a monotonic decreasing stack of indices. Signal: "the next higher one". When a day pops, its answer is i - j, so store indices.

C. A printer gets jobs from many people. Print them in the order they arrived.

No: this is a queue. First in, first out. A stack would print the newest job first. In Python use collections.deque with popleft(); list.pop(0) costs O(n) each time.

D. A web page has tags like <b>, <i> and their closing tags. Check that they nest properly.

Yes, matching pairs. It is the bracket check with longer brackets: push each opening tag, and a closing tag must match the top.

E. Given daily step counts, find the largest total over any 7 days in a row.

No: sliding window. A fixed run of back-to-back days. Add the day that enters, subtract the day that leaves. Nothing waits for a later answer.

F. Bars of a skyline are given. For each bar, find the nearest bar to its left that is shorter.

Yes, a monotonic increasing stack. Pop while the top is not shorter; whatever is on top after that is the answer for the new bar. Then push it. This is the heart of the largest-rectangle problem.

G. A string like 2[ab]3[c] means "ab" twice then "c" three times. Expand it, with nesting allowed.

Yes, the expression variant. On [ push the text so far and the repeat count; on ] pop them and glue the repeated piece on. The newest open bracket closes first.

Trigger signalsOpen and close in pairs, undo, nesting, "next greater or smaller", "days until", spans, histograms, postfix expressions. Not for "in arrival order" (queue) or "a run of k in a row" (window).

6. Classic traps

Trap 1: popping an empty stack

A string that starts with a closer has nothing to match. Without the empty check, Python crashes.

if stack.pop() != pairs[ch]:            # wrong
if not stack or stack.pop() != pairs[ch]: # fixed: check first

# measure.py on ')('
no empty check -> IndexError: pop from empty list
fixed          -> False
Trap 2: forgetting the leftovers

Reaching the end is not enough. Openers still on the stack were never closed.

return True        # wrong:  '((' -> True
return not stack   # fixed:  '((' -> False

For monotonic stacks, leftovers are items that never found an answer. Make sure the default (-1 or 0) is already in place, or flush them with a sentinel.

Trap 3: storing values when you need indices

"How many days until warmer?" needs the distance i - j. If the stack holds temperatures, you have to search for the index again, and repeated values point to the wrong day.

stack.append(t); j = temps.index(v)    # wrong: index() finds the first copy
stack.append(i); j = stack.pop()       # fixed: keep the index itself

# measure.py on [3, 1, 1, 4]
values on the stack  -> [3, 2, 0, 0]
indices on the stack -> [3, 2, 1, 0]

Both 1s have value 1, so index(1) returns 1 twice. Day 2 never gets its answer. The index call is also O(n), which makes the whole thing O(n2).

Trap 4: < or <= with equal values

"Next greater" means strictly greater. If equal values also pop, an item can be "answered" by its twin.

while stack and nums[stack[-1]] <= x:   # wrong for strictly greater
while stack and nums[stack[-1]] < x:    # fixed

# measure.py on [2, 2, 3]
with <= -> [2, 3, -1]
with <  -> [3, 3, -1]

Some problems want <= (for example "span of days with price at most today"). Read the wording, then test with a tie.

Trap 5: operand order and Python's division

In an expression, the first pop is the right-hand number. And Python's // rounds down, not toward zero.

# measure.py
'9 4 -' with swapped pops -> -5     correct -> 5
'7 -2 /' with //          -> -4     with int(a / b) -> -3
Rule to rememberCheck before you pop. Decide what leftovers mean. Store indices. Test a tie, an empty input and a negative division.
Maths

7. Why the monotonic stack is O(n), even with a loop inside a loop

The template has a while inside a for. That looks like O(n2). It is not, and interviewers love to ask why.

Step 1: count pushes and pops

Each index is pushed exactly once: n pushes. An index can only be popped after it was pushed, and it never comes back. So there are at most n pops in the whole run, no matter how they are spread out.

One arrival might pop 999 items at once. But then those 999 are gone, and no later arrival can pop them again. This style of counting is called amortized analysis.

Step 2: count comparisons

Every check of nums[stack[-1]] < x either pops (true) or ends that while (false). A false check happens at most once per arrival. So:

comparisons <= pops + (n - 1) <= (n - 1) + (n - 1) = 2n - 2

The last index is pushed last and never popped, so pops are at most n - 1. On the falling line with n = 1000, measure.py counts 999 pops and 1997 comparisons, just under the bound of 1998. The nested scan does 1000 x 999 / 2 = 499,500.

Step 3: memory and hidden costs

Extra space is O(n): on a falling line nobody ever leaves, so the stack holds everyone until the end.

A Python list is a fine stack: append and pop() at the end are O(1) amortized. The traps are at the other end: pop(0) and insert(0, x) shift every item, O(n) each. Calling list.index or min(stack) inside the loop also hides an O(n) scan.

Costs at a glanceMatching and expressions: O(n) time, O(n) space. Monotonic stack: O(n) time (at most 2n - 2 comparisons), O(n) space. Min stack: O(1) per operation, O(n) space.

Check yourself

9. Your lesson's questions

Q1. Run Template B on [6, 2, 4, 1, 5]. What is the answer list, and which indices are still on the stack at the end?

Answer [-1, 4, 5, 5, -1]; indices 0 and 4 are left. 4 pops the 2; 5 pops the 1 and then the 4; nothing is taller than 6, and nothing comes after 5. measure.py counts 3 pops and 7 comparisons.

Q2. Is ([)] valid? At which character does the bracket check stop?

Not valid; it stops at the third character, ). The top of the stack is [, but ) needs (. Each kind is balanced on its own, which is why counting brackets is not enough. Try it in the sandbox.

Q3. One arrival in a list of 1000 pops 999 items. Can the monotonic stack still be O(n)? What is the most pops in the whole run?

Yes. At most 999 pops in total (n - 1). Each index is pushed once and popped at most once, and the last index is never popped. If one arrival pops 999, every other arrival pops none. measure.py on the falling line: 999 pops, 1997 comparisons.

Glossary: new words from this lesson

stack
A collection you only touch at the top: push to add, pop to remove.
LIFO
Last in, first out: the newest item leaves first.
push / pop / peek
Add on top / remove from the top / look at the top. In Python: append, pop(), stack[-1].
monotonic stack
A stack kept sorted from bottom to top by popping whatever would break the order before each push.
next greater element
For each position, the first strictly larger item to its right.
amortized
Cost averaged over a whole run: a single step can be big, the total stays O(n).
reverse Polish notation
Operators come after their two numbers, as in 3 4 +.
sentinel
A fake edge item, such as a 0 bar at the end, that forces the stack to empty.
queue
The opposite order: first in, first out. Use collections.deque.

Recap: the pattern card in one minute

Idea: keep unfinished items on a stack; the newest one is settled first. A monotonic stack keeps only items that can still get an answer, in sorted order.

Signals: pairs that open and close, undo, nesting, "next greater or smaller", "days until", spans, histograms, postfix expressions.

Template: for each i, x: while stack and a[stack[-1]] < x: j = stack.pop(), answer[j] = x; then stack.append(i). Brackets: push openers, a closer must match the top, return not stack.

Cost: O(n) time and O(n) space. Each index is pushed once and popped at most once: 1997 comparisons against 499,500 for the nested scan at n = 1000.

Traps: popping an empty stack; ignoring leftovers; values instead of indices; < versus <= on ties; pop order and // in expressions.
